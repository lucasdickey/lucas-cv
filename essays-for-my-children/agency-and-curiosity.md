# Agency, Curiosity, and the World You're Walking Into

Colette,

When we were looking at the IB program at Mount Rainier, and then at schools abroad and colleges here, we kept circling back to the same worry: what if AI takes all the jobs? It's a fair question, and honestly it's one I think about every day for work. I'm not going to tell you everything will just work out on its own. But I will tell you what I've seen up close, at my old job and with the people I'm about to work with at my new one.

The people who do really well are not always the smartest people in the room. They're the ones with agency. That's a big word for a simple idea: you decide something is worth doing, and you go do it, without waiting for someone to hand it to you as an assignment. My team at one of my startups used to call me the "King of Hacks," and it wasn't because I was the best engineer. It was because I'd find some way to get the thing done.

Here's why that matters more now than it ever has. For most of history, knowing things was the scarce part. The person who had memorized the facts had the edge. Now AI can answer almost any question in a few seconds. When answers get cheap, the value moves to everything around the answer: knowing what's worth asking, what's worth building, what's worth caring about, and then actually doing it.

That's true whether you end up in science, writing, law, marketing, economics, or something that doesn't have a name yet. In every one of those fields, the person who stands out is the one who's endlessly curious about how the world works, who keeps looking for a better way, who thinks bigger than what's right in front of them, and who then turns around and does it.

You ask a lot of questions, and that's a gift. I want to nudge you toward a particular kind. Some questions are logistical: what time, which form, how many pages. Those are fine. They get you through the day. Other questions go straight to the heart of the thing: Why does this matter? What's actually true here? If I started from scratch, how would I do it?

There's a name for that second way of thinking. It's called first principles thinking. Instead of starting from how everyone else does something, you break the problem down to the few things you know for sure are true, and you build your own answer up from there.

Before I ever worked in tech, I studied philosophy, and it turned out to be one of the most useful things I ever learned, mostly because it trained me to keep asking "why" until I hit bottom. So the next time you catch yourself asking a "what time" question, see if there's a "why" question hiding behind it.

That same habit is how you build your own worldview instead of borrowing someone else's. AI is an incredible tool, and I want you using it all the time. But don't take its answer as the final word. Push back on it, ask it why, check it against what you already know, and then decide for yourself. The same goes for teachers, for the internet, and honestly, for your parents too.

Don't wait for us to suggest a book, or an app you could build, or something new to try. Independence, autonomy, and agency are really three sides of the same thing, and the sooner you start practicing them, the more natural they become. You don't need anyone's permission to be curious. You don't need permission to start.

There's also a big difference between learning about something and doing it. You can read ten articles about how to make an app, or you can build a clumsy one this weekend and a better one next month. The second path teaches you more, every single time.

Almost everyone I admire got where they are by trying a lot of things, plenty of which didn't work, and taking something useful away from each one. That's what carpe diem, seize the day, really means. It isn't about being reckless. It's about noticing an opening and stepping through it before you've talked yourself out of it. Don't ask, just do.

None of this means you have to give up YouTube, Netflix, Pinterest, or the games you like. I watch plenty myself, and rest matters. But it's worth noticing the difference between watching and making.

Scrolling Pinterest for room ideas is fun, and actually redesigning your room is more fun. Watching a baking video is fine, and baking the thing for your friends is better. Listening to a song is great, and learning to play or sing it and recording your own version is something else entirely. Playing a game is a good time, and building a tiny game of your own with Claude might be an even better one.

Those things are just as entertaining, and they leave you with something: a skill, something you made, and a clearer sense of what you like and who you are. Some of them might turn into a lifelong passion. A few might even turn into a career.

So I don't think this is a scary time to be growing up. I think it might be the best time ever to be a curious person with agency, because the tools you have are amazing. Someone your age can now research, write, build, and create things that would have taken a whole team when I was starting out.

What will set you apart isn't something a machine can hand you. It's your curiosity, your taste, and your willingness to start. You already have more of all three than you think, and I can't wait to see what you go do with them. Soon, let's sit down together and map the things you love right now to where they could lead.

Love,
Dad
